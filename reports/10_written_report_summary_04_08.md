# Written Report Summary 04-08

The second half of the pipeline was designed to solve a translation problem between two domains: spatial simulation by tile and the botanical catalog of indoor species. The objective was not to predict species directly from geometry, but to build a methodologically defensible sequence that connects both worlds without losing interpretability.

In notebook 04, the `house_plants.csv` catalog was transformed into an encoded environmental table. The textual description of light, use, and care was translated into continuous and discrete proxies, sufficient to capture behavioral differences between species. On this representation, two clustering options were evaluated, `k=6` and `k=7`, and `k=7` was selected because it improved both inertia and average separation (`silhouette score`). The result was not interpreted as an absolute botanical taxonomy, but as a set of operational environmental profiles.

However, the later analysis showed that several of those botanical clusters, although different inside the catalog, were practically equivalent when observed through the available geometric variables. For that reason, an additional layer was introduced: the `bridge clusters`. This consolidation reduces the number of profiles that the system needs to distinguish in the supervised stage and improves pipeline stability without losing the link to the richness of the original catalog.

In notebook 05, the supervised bridge label between geometry and plants was built. Each cell was compared with the cluster profiles using estimated sun hours and a synthetic light index, producing a top 1 assignment accompanied by probabilities and margins against alternatives. This label is not arbitrary: it is a hybrid, interpretable, and traceable layer generated from soft compatibility rules. Several supervised models were compared on this target, and the best was `RandomForest`, with nearly perfect `accuracy` and `macro_f1`. Methodologically, this supports the claim that `KMeans` and `RandomForest` do not compete: the first organizes the botanical universe; the second learns to recognize that organization from geometry.

In notebook 06, the `bridge cluster` prediction was converted into a top 5 species recommendation. The ranking does not depend only on the predicted cluster name, but also on cluster probability, light fit, and secondary factors such as indoor use, flexibility, and thermal context. In the current results, `Peperomia obtusifolia` and `Peperomia clusiifolia` dominate much of the top 1 recommendations, indicating that an important fraction of the dataset falls into the same operational bridge cluster. That concentration does not invalidate the method; rather, it shows that the dataset's spatial structure clearly favors certain botanical profiles.

In notebook 07, the final output was consolidated into an exportable CSV. This artifact integrates the original spatial class, the target bridge cluster, the bridge cluster predicted by the model, and the top 5 species ranking by row. The result, with 43,100 rows and 55 columns, should no longer be read as a partial prototype, but as a pipeline product sufficiently complete for academic validation and external integration.

Finally, notebook 08 serves a dual function. On one hand, it validates the behavior of the selected model by showing its prediction distribution, confusion matrix, and relative feature importance. On the other, it explains how to reuse the system with new simulated tiles, especially in an environment such as Grasshopper. That transition to deployment is possible precisely because the pipeline ends in a stable supervised model and an externalizable botanical ranking rule.

Taken together, notebooks 04-08 solve the problem in stages: first they build botanical structure, then they translate it into a learnable spatial label, and finally they convert it into a concrete recommendation. That sequence is what allows the system to be defended not as a black box, but as an interpretable recommendation tool assisted by simulation.
